1. Start with the clause

Sentence types describe how clauses fit together. A clause contains a subject and a verb. The subject names who or what the clause is about; the verb expresses an action or state.

Independent clause

The library stays open.

This can stand alone as a complete sentence.

Dependent clause

Because the library stays open

This has a subject and verb, but because makes it depend on another clause: what happens because the library stays open?

In both examples, the subject is the library and the verb is stays. Bold and underlining also mark these parts visually. A group such as after class is a phrase, not a clause: it has no subject–verb pair.

2. Emphasize a point with a simple sentence

A simple sentence has exactly one independent clause and no dependent clauses. “Simple” describes its structure, not its length or the difficulty of its idea.

A short simple sentence can bring a point into focus. Longer simple sentences can gather details around one main statement. Choose the length and rhythm that fit the moment in your paragraph.

Maya reads.

Maya and Luis read.

Maya reads and takes notes.

All three are simple sentences. A clause can have a compound subject (Maya and Luis) or a compound predicate, meaning two verbs sharing a subject (reads and takes). The word and alone does not make a sentence compound.

Comma habit: ordinarily, do not put a comma between a subject and its verb or between two verbs sharing a subject.

3. Connect equally weighted ideas with a compound sentence

A compound sentence has two or more independent clauses and no dependent clauses. Each clause could stand alone, but joining them can show a relationship.

Coordination gives each clause independent status. But sets ideas against each other; so presents a result; and adds another point. Changing the connector changes what you tell your reader.

Independent clause , + FANBOYS independent clause.

[The room was warm], so [Luis opened the window].

Both bracketed parts can stand alone. So presents the second as a result of the first.

FANBOYS helps you remember the seven coordinating conjunctions. Choose one for its meaning, not just because it fits the pattern.

FANBOYS: connections between ideas
WordCommon relationshipExample
ForReason or explanationWe went inside, for the rain had begun.
AndAdditionNia brought rice, and Omar made soup.
NorAnother negative ideaI did not call, nor did I send a message.
ButContrastThe path was steep, but we kept walking.
OrAlternativeWe can cook, or we can order food.
YetUnexpected contrastThe bag was small, yet it held everything.
SoResultThe store was closed, so we went home.

For as a conjunction sounds more formal than because. After nor, notice the order did I. These words have other uses too: for dinner does not join clauses.

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction when it joins independent clauses. Compare: Nia cooked and washed the dishes. One shared subject, so no comma is needed.

Another way to make a compound sentence

A semicolon can join closely related independent clauses: The store was closed; we went home. A comma alone cannot do this in standard edited prose.

However is not a FANBOYS conjunction. Write The store was closed; however, the café was open. You can also use a period before However.

4. Frame a main point with a complex sentence

A complex sentence has exactly one independent clause and at least one dependent clause. A dependent clause can explain a reason, set a condition, establish time, or introduce a contrast.

Which idea gets the emphasis?

Although the room was warm, Luis kept working.
The main statement is that Luis kept working; the heat becomes background contrast.

Although Luis kept working, the room was warm.
Now the main statement concerns the heat. The grammar works, but the relationship may feel less natural. Choose a main clause and a connection that match the point you want to make.

Subordinators, also called subordinating conjunctions, introduce dependent clauses. Here are common ones:

  • Reason: because, since
  • Time: when, while, before, after, until, as soon as
  • Condition: if, unless
  • Contrast: although, even though, whereas

Meaning depends on context. Since can mark time or a reason. Also compare after lunch (a phrase) with after we ate lunch (a clause).

Dependent clause first

[Because the room was warm], [Luis opened the window].

Use a comma after an introductory dependent clause.

Independent clause first

[Luis opened the window] [because the room was warm].

Usually, no comma separates the main clause from a following dependent clause that completes its meaning.

The second rule is a starting point, not a ban on all commas before dependent clauses. A clause added as an aside or a strong contrast may take a comma.

Can a dependent clause describe a noun?

Yes. In The neighbor who lives upstairs plays guitar, who lives upstairs is a dependent relative clause. It identifies the neighbor. The sentence is complex even without a word such as because.

Use commas around added information, but not information needed to identify whom or what you mean: My neighbor Leila, who lives upstairs, plays guitar. Here the relative clause adds information about an already identified person.

5. Combine moves with a compound-complex sentence

A compound-complex sentence has at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

[Although the room was warm], [Luis kept working], but [Nia took a break].

  • Dependent: Although the room was warm
  • Independent: Luis kept working
  • Independent: Nia took a break

The first comma closes the introductory dependent clause. The second comma goes before but, which joins two independent clauses. You are applying two familiar rules in one sentence.

6. Use commas to show structure

Use the relationships between words and clauses to decide where commas belong. A pause while reading is not a dependable rule by itself.

  • Two independent clauses + FANBOYS: I checked the address, but I missed the turn.
  • Introductory dependent clause: If you need directions, call me.
  • A series of three or more: Bring paper, pencils, and a folder. This lesson uses the final serial comma; some styles omit it.
  • Added information: My bicycle, a gift from my aunt, needs new tires. Use both commas when the added information is in the middle.

Avoid a comma splice: The café closed, we went home. Both sides are independent. Repair it with a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a suitable coordinating conjunction: The café closed, so we went home.

Avoid a fragment in a complete-sentence task: Because the café closed. Attach that dependent clause to an independent clause: Because the café closed, we went home. These guidelines describe standard edited prose; writers sometimes use fragments deliberately in other contexts.

7. Try the moves as a writer

Use these ideas: The rain stopped. We walked to the park. The grass was wet. Try the four moves below. You may use only the ideas you need for the first three; use all three for the last.

Read each version aloud and notice how the emphasis and rhythm change. Compare with the sample after you try your own. More than one response can work; the page does not automatically evaluate your writing.

Compare with one possible response

We walked to the park.

One independent clause, no dependent clauses. You do not need to use all three ideas.

Compare with one possible response

The rain stopped, so we walked to the park.

Two independent clauses. The comma plus so connects them and signals a result.

Compare with one possible response

After the rain stopped, we walked to the park.

After the rain stopped is dependent. We walked to the park is independent. The opening clause takes a comma.

Compare with one possible response

After the rain stopped, we walked to the park, but the grass was wet.

The opening After clause is dependent. We walked to the park and the grass was wet are independent. Each comma has a different job.

You do not need all four sentence types in every paragraph. If you do not have a draft with you, write a short paragraph about an everyday activity and revise two sentences from it.

Before you finish

Choose the revision that best serves your paragraph. What gets the emphasis? Does the connecting word express the relationship you mean? Read it aloud for rhythm, then check its clause boundaries and punctuation.
